Design a marketing website for Orla, a Federal BD Intelligence Platform. Single-page site, scroll-driven, 8 sections. The audience is federal business development professionals at small and mid-size GovCon firms — people with 5 to 10 hours a week for BD analysis who currently do it in spreadsheets, in their head, or by paying consultants $150–300 per hour.

Positioning
Orla is not a dashboard, not a CRM, and not a search tool. It is the work product of a senior analyst who has spent the week researching the user's firm and markets. Competitors: GovWin IQ (~$29K/year, a search database), HigherGov (~$500+/year, a cheaper search database), GovDash (post-decision proposal workflow). None of them do the strategic analysis between "here's the data" and "now go bid." That gap is Orla's market.

Visual language
Ground: dark navy across the entire site #0F1B2D — this is the dark navy in the attached Orla logo.
Text on dark: warm off-white for body (~#FAFAF9), slightly muted for secondary text.
Brand accent: the gold in the logo dot (~#B8860B, Orla Gold). Used surgically — the logo, primary CTAs, key brand moments. Never decorative. Never in data or scoring contexts.
Example-intelligence scoring colors (only where Orla's actual output is shown): emerald #059669 for strong / positive, amber #D97706 for moderate / tension, crimson #DC2626 for alerts / negative.

Typography:
- DM Sans for all prose, headings, UI. Weights 400, 500, 600, and 700 for hero-scale display type.
- IBM Plex Mono for every number — dollar values, percentages, scores, counts, confidence intervals — including inline within DM Sans prose paragraphs. This inline mono-in-sans pattern is Orla's signature typographic move and must appear visibly on the site.

Aesthetic: "Quiet Authority." Premium dossier, not SaaS landing page. Layered depth, typography-driven structure, generous whitespace, institutional feel. Think McKinsey / Bloomberg / The Economist — not Vercel / Linear / Stripe.

Motion: heavier than a typical product interior is acceptable here — scroll-driven section reveals, animated data visualizations in hero, subtle 3D elements (tasteful, not showy). Gold can appear in motion moments. Same rule applies: motion communicates, never entertains. No parallax for its own sake.

Voice
Declarative. Quantified. Specific. Every claim includes a number. Every number has context.
- Yes: "$142M in DoD IT services annually, ranked #3 of 73 agencies, growing 11% YoY"
- No: "significant federal opportunities"
- Yes: "Your top 3 agencies, ranked, with the one insight that matters"
- No: "unlock your federal potential"

No exclamation marks. No emoji. No "great news." Warm but not casual. Think senior analyst at a boutique consulting firm, not a marketing copywriter.

Page structure
1. Hero. One-sentence promise + one supporting line + primary CTA ("Start your 14-day trial"). Background visual: a hero-scale mock of an Orla Strategy Brief — real-looking intelligence copy with IBM Plex Mono numbers, a partial agency ranking bar chart behind the type. Not a product screenshot — an evocation.
2. What's broken today. Three specific pains: "You spend Friday afternoons comparing NAICS spend across agencies in spreadsheets," "You pay a consultant $200/hour to tell you which recompetes matter," "GovWin IQ costs $29,000 and still hands you a search box." Named, not vague.
3. How Orla is different. The 80/20 philosophy. Briefing-first design (narrative, then evidence). The three-zone framework — Rules (stated as fact), Patterns (stated as informed assessment with data basis), Judgment (named, not predicted). An excerpted Strategy Brief paragraph demonstrates the voice.
4. Example intelligence. Full-width mock of one Strategy Brief section: "Your top agencies, ranked" — narrative opener, horizontal bar chart, inline quantified annotations. Shows the product without requiring signup.
5. Pricing. Three tiers: Starter, Professional, Team. Differentiated by analytical depth (template voice vs. full prepared-analyst narrative), factor count surfaced, and seat count — not by feature checklists. Prices as placeholders: "$X / month."
6. Who it's for. Four personas, one paragraph each: solo founder running a small GovCon firm, part-time BD lead, full-time BD professional at a mid-size firm, Team tier for coordinated BD groups of 3–10.
7. Privacy and trust. Cancel anytime. 60-day data retention after cancel, then permanent deletion. Comprehensive data export available anytime. No re-engagement emails after cancellation. These are a full section, not fine-print — small federal firms have been burned by software vendors, and stating this plainly is a trust move.
8. Footer. Wordmark, standard links, nothing else. No social-media sprawl.

What to avoid
- Dashboard screenshots. Orla is not a dashboard.
- Stock photography of people in suits pointing at laptops.
- Generic SaaS gradients, glass morphism, purple-to-pink hero washes.
- Testimonial carousels with three-line quotes.
- "Trusted by" logo walls.
- The phrase "AI-powered" anywhere on the site.
- Gold anywhere outside the brand-moment allowlist (logo, CTAs, occasional accent).
